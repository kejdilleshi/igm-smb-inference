\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Overview}\label{sec:overview}

We recover the surface mass balance (SMB) of a glacier with a two-step pipeline,
implemented as a process module within the open-source Instructed Glacier Model
\citep[IGM;][]{jouvet2023, jouvet_cordonnier2023}. The forward problem is governed
by depth-integrated mass conservation,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial H}{\partial t} = b - \nabla\cdot\mathbf{q},
  \qquad
  \mathbf{q} = H\,\bar{\mathbf{u}}(H, z_s, c),
  \label{eq:masscons}
\end{equation}
where $H$ is the ice thickness, $b$ the SMB, $z_s = z_b + H$ the surface elevation,
$c$ the basal sliding coefficient, and $\bar{\mathbf{u}}$ the depth-averaged
horizontal velocity returned by IGM's deep-learning ice-flow emulator
\citep{jouvet_cordonnier2023}.

Equation~\eqref{eq:masscons} makes the central difficulty explicit: the geometry
evolution depends jointly on the unknown SMB $b$ and on the ice dynamics through
the flux $\mathbf{q}$, which itself depends on $H$ and $c$. Inferring $b$ from an
observed geometry change therefore requires the dynamical state to be fixed
beforehand; otherwise an error in the dynamics would be aliased into the inferred
SMB. Our pipeline reflects this dependence directly (Fig.~\ref{fig:setup}):
(i)~we first calibrate the dynamical state by data assimilation against a
surface-velocity snapshot near the start of the inversion window---inverting the
ice-thickness field $H$, while the sliding and Arrhenius factor are fixed by an
ensemble search---augmented on Argentière, where extra observations allow the
sliding coefficient to be inverted as a field (Sect.~\ref{sec:da}); (ii)~holding the
dynamics fixed, we then infer the SMB. Starting from the first surface DEM, we
integrate \eqref{eq:masscons} forward to the epoch of the second and seek the
elevation-dependent SMB profile whose simulated geometry best matches the observed
one (Sect.~\ref{sec:inference}).

\subsection{Ice-dynamics calibration via data assimilation}\label{sec:da}

The calibration seeks the ice-thickness field that, when passed through the
ice-flow emulator, best reproduces the observed surface velocities. We use IGM's
existing data-assimilation module \citep{jouvet2023} to invert for the thickness
$H$ by minimising the regularised misfit
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{DA}}(H) =
  \alpha_u \big\|\mathbf{u}_s(H) - \mathbf{u}_s^{\mathrm{obs}}\big\|^2
  + \alpha_H \big\|H - H^{\mathrm{obs}}\big\|^2_{\Omega_{\mathrm{thk}}}
  + \mathcal{R}(H),
  \label{eq:da}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{u}_s(H)$ is the modelled surface velocity returned by the emulator,
the second term penalises departure from point ice-thickness measurements over
their survey footprint $\Omega_{\mathrm{thk}}$, and $\mathcal{R}$ is a
Tikhonov-style smoothness regulariser on $H$. On Argentière the thickness
measurements are ground-penetrating-radar (GPR) soundings \citep{rabatel2018}; for
glaciers without such surveys (Great Aletsch, Rhône) the second term is dropped and
the calibration is governed by the surface-velocity misfit and the regulariser
alone. The minimisation is solved by L-BFGS; we refer the reader to
\citet{jouvet2023} for the full formulation, weighting strategy and convergence
behaviour.

On the two velocity-only glaciers (Great Aletsch, Rhône) the basal sliding
coefficient $c$ is \emph{not} inverted pointwise. The emulated flow regime is
instead set by two scalar physical parameters---$c$ and the ice rate (Arrhenius)
factor $A$---fixed beforehand by an ensemble search: candidate $(c, A)$ pairs are
each passed through the data assimilation, and we retain the pair that minimises the
surface-velocity misfit, so the assimilation itself inverts the thickness $H$ alone.

On Argentière the GPR soundings supply an independent thickness constraint that
makes a joint inversion identifiable, so we additionally relax $c$ into a spatial
field and invert it alongside $H$ (the Arrhenius factor remains the scalar set by
the ensemble search). This is also motivated physically: \citet{kneib2024} find that
the spatial variability of the sliding coefficient helps explain the observed
surface velocities on these glaciers, and the recovered field $c_{\mathrm{DA}}$
reproduces the expected structure (Sect.~\ref{sec:argentiere-da}). In either case
the smoothness weight in $\mathcal{R}$ is set at the corner of the resulting L-curve,
and only once the calibrated state matches the observed velocity---and the GPR
thickness, where available---to within observational uncertainty do we freeze the
calibrated state and proceed to the SMB inversion.

\subsection{SMB inference by automatic differentiation}\label{sec:inference}

\paragraph{Observation target.}
With the dynamics frozen, the inversion is driven by the ice-thickness field at the
end of the window, $T$. From the calibrated thickness we fix the bedrock,
\begin{equation}
  z_b(\mathbf{x}) = z_s^{\mathrm{obs}}(\mathbf{x}, t_0) - H_{\mathrm{DA}}(\mathbf{x}),
  \label{eq:bed}
\end{equation}
and, assuming $z_b$ does not evolve over $[t_0, T]$---justified on decadal
timescales---combine it with the observed end-of-window surface DEM to obtain the
target
\begin{equation}
  H^{\mathrm{obs}}(\mathbf{x}, T) = z_s^{\mathrm{obs}}(\mathbf{x}, T) - z_b(\mathbf{x}).
  \label{eq:hobs}
\end{equation}
The two surface DEMs $z_s^{\mathrm{obs}}(\cdot, t_0)$ and
$z_s^{\mathrm{obs}}(\cdot, T)$ are the accurate, glacier-specific two-epoch
elevation models described in Sect.~\ref{sec:results}; their difference defines the
geometry change the inversion must reproduce.

\paragraph{Forward model.}
With the dynamical state frozen at $H_{\mathrm{DA}}$ and the flow scalars
$(c, A)$, the glacier evolves according to \eqref{eq:masscons}, which we integrate
explicitly with a forward-Euler scheme,
\begin{equation}
  H^{n+1} = \max\!\big(0,\; H^n + \Delta t^n [\,b^n - \nabla\cdot\mathbf{q}^n\,]\big),
  \label{eq:euler}
\end{equation}
where the non-negativity projection prevents spurious negative thicknesses near the
margin. The step is limited by a Courant--Friedrichs--Lewy condition,
$\Delta t^n = c_{\mathrm{cfl}}\,\Delta x / \max_\Omega\|\bar{\mathbf{u}}^n\|$, with
$c_{\mathrm{cfl}} = 0.2$, and the flux divergence is evaluated with a centred
finite-difference stencil on the staggered grid. Because $\bar{\mathbf{u}}$ is
recomputed from the current $(H, z_s)$ at every step, the flux divergence evolves
with the geometry rather than being held at its initial-snapshot value---the
property that removes the stationary-flux bias of geodetic flux-divergence
inversions \citep{miles2021, kneib2024}.

\paragraph{SMB parametrisation.}
The unknown is a one-dimensional SMB profile $\beta(z)$, a function of elevation
only, discretised at $N$ uniformly spaced elevation bins by the values
$\mathbf{m} = (m_1, \ldots, m_N)$. At each time step it is mapped to the 2D source
term in \eqref{eq:masscons} by evaluating it at the current surface elevation,
$b(\mathbf{x}, t) = \beta(z_s(\mathbf{x}, t))$; the interpolation is
piecewise-linear and differentiable everywhere with respect to $\mathbf{m}$, so
gradients propagate cleanly through every time step. As argued in
Sect.~\ref{sec:contribution}, collapsing the unknown from $\mathcal{O}(10^4)$
pixels to $N = \mathcal{O}(10)$ bins reflects the first-order elevation control on
alpine SMB and regularises the otherwise severely ill-posed inversion.

\paragraph{Inverse problem.}
We seek the profile $\mathbf{m}^\star$ that minimises the misfit between the
simulated end-of-window thickness $H^{\mathrm{sim}}(\cdot, T; \mathbf{m})$ and the
target $H^{\mathrm{obs}}(\cdot, T)$ from \eqref{eq:hobs}:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{J}(\mathbf{m}) =
  \sqrt{\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega}
    \big(H^{\mathrm{sim}} - H^{\mathrm{obs}}\big)^2}
  \;+\; \mathcal{R}(\mathbf{m}),
  \label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
where the first term is the RMS geometry misfit and
$\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{m})$ is
a Tikhonov-style smoothness penalty on the squared discrete curvature of the
profile; $\lambda$ trades data fidelity against
smoothness. The minimisation uses L-BFGS \citep{liunocedal1989} with $M = 10$ correction pairs and
a Hager--Zhang line search; the low dimensionality of $\mathbf{m}$ makes the
implicit Hessian approximation accurate, and convergence is reached in
$\mathcal{O}(10^2)$ function evaluations. The gradient
$\nabla_{\mathbf{m}}\mathcal{J}$ is obtained by reverse-mode automatic
differentiation \citep{paszke2017} through the entire forward integration
\eqref{eq:masscons}--\eqref{eq:euler}: every operation in the forward map---the
emulator evaluation, the staggered-grid divergence, the explicit Euler update, the
elevation-based interpolation, and the cost itself---is a differentiable TensorFlow
operation, so the chain rule applies uniformly across the forward graph. The
principal limitation is memory: a naïve AD trace stores the full computational
graph, whose footprint scales as $\mathcal{O}(N_t \cdot N_\Omega)$ with $N_t$ time
steps and $N_\Omega$ grid points, exceeding the budget of a single GPU. We address
this with gradient checkpointing \citep{griewank2000}: the integration is
partitioned into $K = \sqrt{N_t}$ segments, only the segment-boundary states are
stored during the forward pass, and the within-segment graphs are reconstructed
during the backward pass, reducing the memory cost from $\mathcal{O}(N_t)$ to
$\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{N_t})$ at the price of one additional forward evaluation per
backward pass.
