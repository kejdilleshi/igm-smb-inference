\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Mountain glaciers are sensitive recorders of climate and a critical component of
regional water resources, yet projecting their evolution requires their
distributed surface mass balance (SMB)---the spatial pattern of accumulation and
ablation---which remains poorly constrained at the global scale. Direct, in situ
measurements rely on stake networks and snow pits that are labour-intensive,
hazardous in steep accumulation areas, and deployed on fewer than 0.2\,\% of the
world's glacierised area \citep{cogley2011}. Regional and global climate models
provide continuous gridded SMB but reproduce glacier-scale mass change only after
calibration \citep{radic2014, marzeion2012}. Most glaciers therefore remain
effectively unmonitored, and regional-to-global mass-balance assessments depend on
extrapolation from a few benchmark sites or on model reconstructions
\citep{hugonnet2021, zemp2019}. As remote-sensing products of glacier surface
velocity \citep{millan2022}, ice thickness \citep{farinotti2019, millan2022} and
elevation change \citep{hugonnet2021, beraud2023} reach near-global coverage, the
question is no longer whether the data exist, but how to combine them to infer
physically consistent SMB fields.

\subsection{Existing approaches}\label{sec:sota}

SMB can be derived directly from climate models that simulate surface energy
balance and precipitation, but their coarse resolution and simplified treatment of
high-mountain microclimate bias precipitation phase, accumulation gradients and
melt, so they rarely reproduce observed glacier change without additional bias
correction \citep{marzeion2012, rounce2020}. Alternatively, sparse direct
measurements are extended to distributed fields by homogenising them against
decadal geodetic volume change, as in the long-running Glacier Monitoring
Switzerland programme \citep[GLAMOS;][]{glamos}; this yields a valuable
observational reference, but a fundamentally different product from the transient
inversion proposed here.

The route most closely related to the present work exploits the mass-conservation
equation, which relates the rate of surface-elevation change to the divergence of
the ice flux and the SMB \citep{cuffey2010}. Given ice thickness and surface
velocity, the vertically-integrated flux is computed, differentiated spatially,
and combined with an observed elevation-change field to yield the SMB. From early
flux-gate estimates of emergence velocity in the ablation zone
\citep{berthier2012, brun2018}, the approach has been generalised to fully
distributed SMB over entire glaciers
\citep{bisset2020, miles2021, pelto2021, vantricht2021, kneib2022}. Its
state-of-the-art realisation is that of \citet{kneib2024}, who reconstructed a
high-resolution distributed SMB for Argentière Glacier (French Alps) over
2012--2021 from Pléiades elevation change and velocity, ground-penetrating-radar
thickness and three ice-thickness models. Validation against the GLACIOCLIM stake
network gave root-mean-square errors of 0.50--0.96\,m\,w.e.\,yr$^{-1}$ and
$R^2 > 0.91$, resolving spatial patterns---including avalanche-fed accumulation
hotspots---well beyond what stake networks capture.

Two difficulties beset this route. First, the flux divergence is the spatial
derivative of the product of two independently-measured noisy fields, so it is
dominated by small-scale noise that is suppressed by \emph{post hoc} spatial
filtering \citep{vantricht2021, bisset2020}; yet smoothing a quantity that should
satisfy mass conservation locally redistributes mass between grid cells and
violates the very balance the method enforces. Second, and more fundamentally, the
flux divergence is computed at a single observational snapshot and assumed
representative of the whole observation period, whereas glacier geometry, velocity
and flux all evolve under ongoing climate forcing
\citep{johannesson1989, zekollari2015}; treating the flux as stationary introduces
a bias that grows with the length of the window---a limitation \citet{kneib2024}
acknowledge explicitly. Per-glacier tuning of smoothing length scales, density
assumptions and weighting schemes further hampers transfer to the regional scales
most relevant for water-resource applications.

\subsection{This study}\label{sec:contribution}

Two features distinguish this formulation from flux-divergence inversions. First,
the SMB is recovered by solving the forward mass-conservation equation rather than
by differentiating observed fields: the flux divergence is recomputed at every
time step from the evolving glacier state, so the inferred SMB is dynamically
consistent over the whole window---rather than assuming a stationary flux---and
mass is conserved exactly at every step, with no \emph{post hoc} smoothing of a
noisy divergence field. Second, we parametrise the SMB as a one-dimensional
function of surface elevation, evaluated at every grid cell to yield a 2D field. A
fully distributed SMB has as many unknowns as grid cells, whereas the geometric
constraint supplies at most one scalar per cell---and none where the ice is thin or
absent; collapsing the unknown to an elevation profile reduces the dimensionality
from $\mathcal{O}(10^4)$ to $\mathcal{O}(10)$ and renders the otherwise severely
ill-posed inversion well-constrained, without site-specific smoothing length scales
or density assumptions. The price---horizontal variability within an elevation
band, such as the avalanche-fed hotspots resolved by \citet{kneib2024}, is not
recovered---is a deliberate trade-off of sub-elevation detail for a transferable,
tuning-free inversion, to which we return in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

The accuracy of any geometry-based inversion is ultimately set by the input DEMs.
Near-global elevation-change products \citep{hugonnet2021} make the method broadly
applicable, but their smoothing limits the fidelity of the recovered SMB; dedicated
multi-temporal DEMs deliver the highest accuracy, and the rapidly expanding archive
of such data is what makes a regional-scale application realistic. We therefore
validate the method with accurate two-epoch DEMs on three Alpine glaciers of
contrasting size and data availability: Argentière (French Alps), where a dense
stake network, repeated ground-penetrating-radar surveys and a rich Pléiades
archive allow a direct benchmark against the state-of-the-art reconstruction of
\citet{kneib2024}; and Great Aletsch and Rhône (Swiss Alps), constrained by surface
velocity alone, without any thickness survey, representing the data-sparse regime
typical of most glaciers. Section~\ref{sec:methods} details the two-step
pipeline---data assimilation of the initial dynamical state, then SMB inference by
automatic differentiation through the transient forward run;
Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the inferred SMB profiles and their validation
against in situ stake measurements; and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the
implications for regional-scale SMB inversion.
